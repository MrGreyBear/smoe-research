\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper started from the question whether a heterogeneous mixture-of-experts network can forecast
the CSI~300. Under a leak-free protocol no model in our comparison forecasts daily means or directions,
and the accuracy figures that motivated the original work came from look-ahead. We therefore asked
what a forecaster knows about the conditional distribution of returns and whether that knowledge can
be written down. The Semantic Mixture of Experts answers this for itself in closed form with standard
errors, and for other forecasters through the KL projection.

Five findings carry over beyond this paper. First, price-level losses cannot reveal skill, and the
differenced directional-accuracy metric measures sign persistence in the data rather than anything
about the model (\cref{prop:bound,prop:da}). Second, a three-regime white box with ten covariates
matches the density forecasts of a deep heterogeneous MoE to within eight percent of its gain and
dominates single-regime, Markov-switching, boosted and zero-shot foundation models; most of the value
comes from variance dynamics and mixture shape, and the gate's state dependence adds a smaller,
testable increment. Three experts forecast better than two, four or five, even though in-sample
criteria disagree on the number. Third, the white box produces economic content that a black box
hides: in the CSI~300, volatility rather than momentum or drawdown moves regime weights, the
short-horizon regime continues in China but reverses in developed markets, and the US VIX enters
regime weights in all six international equity indices but in only one of the four A-share indices.
Fourth, how daily variance is measured matters. A noisy estimator shifts weight to longer horizons
by an amount that

\cref{prop:eiv} predicts, part of the mixture's advantage is insurance against that noise, and with
realised variance a single-regime Realized GARCH is the best density forecaster for the CSI~300.
Fifth, risk forecasts are where distributional models pay off (tail calibration, a conformal coverage
guarantee and lower drawdowns), whereas claims of directional alpha should be met with deflated
Sharpe ratios and backtest-overfitting probabilities before anyone acts on them.

The limitations are those of a first step. The model is univariate and daily; its gate covariates,
although now screened by a score test, are drawn from a short list; realised measures are available
to us only for the CSI~300; the foundation models enter zero-shot and through a Gaussian projection of
their quantiles, and the release-date test bounds rather than excludes pretraining look-ahead; and our
economic analysis concerns one market and two instruments. The research agenda of \cref{sec:agenda}
lists the extensions we consider most promising (multivariate and decision-focused SMoEs,
language-model gates, online Bayesian updating, experts with realised-variance recursions and
point-in-time foundation models). Each keeps the property that made this exercise informative: every
statement the model makes comes with a closed form and a standard error that a reader can check.
